## 2c. One operational reversal: the transient row is a nuisance-rejection test

A 6-month surge that a detector declares a *sustained* change is a false-change event operationally — that is what the benchmark's own spike table measures for a one-month spike. Reversing the metric (higher is better: do not declare a change on a temporary surge):

| detector | declares a change | correctly does not declare | naive − candidate | 95% CI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| naive_multiplier | 0.168 | 0.832 | — | — |
| rolling_zscore | 0.029 | 0.971 | +0.139 | [+0.105, +0.177] |
| poisson_cusum | 0.065 | 0.935 | +0.103 | [+0.068, +0.141] |
| poisson_bayes_factor | 0.011 | 0.989 | +0.157 | [+0.119, +0.196] |

This is the one place where the likelihood machinery does what it was designed to do: a temporary surge is not fitted as a persistent split. If a transient is a nuisance to be rejected rather than a target to be detected, the Bayes factor beats the naive comparator on that model, with an interval excluding zero — while the detection-power table above scores the same behaviour as a loss. Both readings are of the same numbers; which one applies is an operational decision, not a statistical one.

## 4. Verdict

**At the pre-registered 2 % false-alarm budget the headline is robust to the injection model.** Under a smooth 12-month ramp, a 6-month transient surge and a bursty 50 %-of-months increase, neither the Poisson CUSUM nor the Bayes factor beats the naive rate-multiplier, and neither beats it at the benchmark's fixed step-model operating points. It is not rescued by a different budget either: across the 1 / 3 / 5 % frontier no likelihood win is CI-confirmed in any injection model (the only positive point estimates are step/poisson_bayes_factor @ 1% +0.024; ramp/poisson_bayes_factor @ 1% +0.024; ramp/poisson_bayes_factor @ 5% +0.011). The one finding that cuts against the headline is the transient model read as a nuisance rather than a target: the Bayes factor declares a sustained change on only 0.011 of 6-month surges versus 0.168 for the naive comparator (naive − Bayes factor +0.157 [+0.119, +0.196], CI excludes zero). So if a temporary surge is a false-change nuisance rather than a signal to detect, a likelihood detector does beat the simple baseline — on that one model and on that reading. Mechanically, the reason is the density of the lane: at ~0.2 events/month even a 3x or 5x rate change adds so few events that the CUSUM's locally re-estimated baseline and target drift, and the Bayes factor's Gamma(1,1) regularisation, give back more than the sharper alternative model can add; the naive comparator's six-events-in-three-months guard plus a rate ratio over a long baseline is simply well matched to this sparsity. Transient changes are the weakest case for every detector and the Bayes factor nearly collapses (it is built to reject a split that does not persist), so a transient is a sensitivity stress test, not a detector endorsement. The correct reading of the published result is therefore narrow and budget-explicit: **at a matched 2 % false alarm budget on this sparse lane, an abrupt, ramped, transient or bursty multiplicative rate increase is not detected better by a Poisson likelihood detector than by the matched simple rate test** — and the report should keep the 2 % qualifier attached to every statement of that claim rather than generalising it to all false-alarm budgets. One separate audit finding: `detect_v1/REPORT.md` §5 states that the naive detector is 'the strongest detector at every reported false-alarm budget', which is contradicted by the benchmark's own read-out (track @ 1%: poisson_bayes_factor 0.122 vs naive 0.098; domain @ 1%: poisson_bayes_factor 0.129 vs naive 0.106; domain @ 3%: poisson_bayes_factor 0.237 vs naive 0.229; domain @ 5%: poisson_bayes_factor 0.237 vs naive 0.229); the 2 % headline is unaffected but that sentence is too strong. Paired differences versus naive at the 2 % budget — Poisson CUSUM {step: -0.042 [-0.070, -0.012], CI excludes 0; ramp: -0.046 [-0.069, -0.022], CI excludes 0; transient: -0.103 [-0.141, -0.068], CI excludes 0; heterogeneous: -0.062 [-0.086, -0.037], CI excludes 0}; Bayes factor {step: -0.101 [-0.134, -0.067], CI excludes 0; ramp: -0.033 [-0.063, -0.003], CI excludes 0; transient: -0.157 [-0.196, -0.119], CI excludes 0; heterogeneous: -0.099 [-0.136, -0.063], CI excludes 0}.

## 5. Limitations of this verification

* The four detector implementations are imported (`medradar.intelligence.changepoint`), not re-derived; a shared implementation bug would cancel out of the reproduction and of the detector comparison. Injection, scoring, false-alarm accounting, the budget selection and the bootstrap are re-implemented here.
* The exact seed hash width/ordering is not documented in the artifacts; the shipped convention was used to make the reproduction exact, and the seed-sensitivity table bounds the dependence on it.
* The 2 % read-out takes the best frontier point inside the budget, which is optimistic in absolute level (but symmetric across detectors). The `Δ fixed` column removes that selection for the headline comparison.
* Detection of a transient injection is credited whenever an upward declaration lands in the window, even during the surge; for a transient the operationally correct behaviour may be *not* to declare a sustained change, so transient P(detection) is a sensitivity statistic, not a quality score by itself.
* Cells are treated as independent except in the bootstrap (which resamples entities); company- or regulator-wide correlated surges are not modelled.
* All injected alternatives are still synthetic Poisson trajectories built from a trailing-12-month local baseline; they do not establish that real technology changes look like any of them.
